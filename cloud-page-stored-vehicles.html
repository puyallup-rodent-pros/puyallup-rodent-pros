A Parked Vehicle Is a Warm, Dry Nest

An RV, boat, or classic car that sits unused for the winter is exactly what rats and mice look for in fall: dry, sheltered, quiet, and full of soft material to nest in. Seat cushions, insulation, wiring covers, and stored bedding all make good nesting. Many owners discover the problem in spring, when they open the door to chewed seats, a nest in the air filter, and an engine that will not start.

Mice enter through gaps you would never notice. A mouse fits through a hole the size of a dime, so any opening around a cable, a vent, a drain, or a door seal is enough. Rats follow the same routes. They climb tires and hoses, cross from a nearby woodpile or shrub, and slip in under a door that does not sit flush. Once inside, they stay, because the vehicle is warm, dark, and undisturbed for months.

The Damage They Cause

Chewed wiring is the most serious damage. Rodents gnaw on wire insulation, and in a vehicle that can mean a dead battery, failed lights, or an engine that will not turn over. In the worst case, exposed wires are a fire risk. They also chew hoses, belts, and plastic fittings, and they shred seat foam and insulation for nesting material. Droppings and urine soak into upholstery and carpet, and the smell can linger long after the animals are gone. A nest in an air vent can block airflow and push droppings into the cabin every time the fan runs.

How to Keep Them Out

Start by taking the food out. Never store snacks, pet food, or bird seed in a parked vehicle, and clean out every crumb before storage. Remove bedding, towels, and paper goods, or seal them in hard plastic bins with tight lids. A vehicle with nothing to eat and nothing soft to nest in is far less attractive.

Seal the entry points. Check door and window seals, and replace any that are cracked or loose. Cover vents and drain openings with quarter inch hardware cloth, fastened so it cannot be pulled free. Stuff steel wool into small gaps around cables and pipes, and seal over it. Do not use spray foam alone, because rodents chew straight through it.

Cut the routes to the vehicle. Move woodpiles, stacked boxes, and dense shrubs away from where the vehicle is parked. Trim branches that hang over it. Park on concrete or gravel rather than grass where possible, because tall grass gives rodents cover right up to the tires. If the vehicle sits on grass, keep the grass cut short around it all winter.

Check it through the winter. Open the vehicle every few weeks, look for droppings, gnaw marks, and nesting material, and listen for movement. A quick check in December catches a problem that would be a ruined spring if it waited until April. Pay special attention to the engine bay, the air filter box, and under the seats.

When the Vehicle Is Already Occupied

If you find droppings, nests, or chewed wiring, do not start the engine until the vehicle has been checked. A nest in the engine bay or the air intake can cause damage the moment the engine turns over. Remove nesting material with gloves, clean the area, and find the entry point before the vehicle goes back into storage. Traps alone will not solve it while the gaps stay open, because new animals arrive to replace the ones that are removed.
